Makerism

I developed Makerism from Generative Sovereignty to connect the work of making and maintaining a country with the ownership and institutions that let its people remain independent.

I developed Makerism from Generative Sovereignty. That philosophy came first. It gave me the basis for drawing older political ideas together and deciding what a political order must preserve if a people is to remain free across generations.

Makerism concerns the work of making and maintaining that order. A country needs people who can grow its food, build its equipment, teach its children, care for its sick, and keep its institutions honest. Their work gives independence a material and moral footing. When the country loses the ability to do these things, its freedom depends increasingly on whoever will do them for it.

Where Makerism sits in the framework

Start with the foundation, then follow the development of Makerism and its relationship with the other philosophies.

Generative Sovereignty came first

I began with a philosophy about how a people sustains the moral and practical conditions of self-government. Virtue has to be taught and practiced. Institutions must preserve the knowledge and relationships on which they depend. The question reaches beyond the people holding power at a particular moment.

That foundation guided the synthesis. Older ideas could be examined for what they contributed, and new philosophies could be developed around responsibilities the whole account needed to carry. Makerism is one result of that work.

Threefold Voice

The work through which a country keeps its independence

Makerism gives attention to the people who create and maintain the means of national life. A farmer and an engineer perform different work, but the country depends on both. Teaching, care, and maintenance belong in the account because production cannot continue without the people and knowledge they preserve.

Ownership gives this work a more durable stake. A household or proprietor should be able to keep and pass on what it has built. The economic arrangements surrounding the work should make that responsibility attainable and leave room for another person to enter it.

Producerism

Distinct arguments brought together through one foundation

Hearthism developed from Generative Sovereignty as a philosophy concerned with the household and the social conditions of freedom. Its account of formation and care gives it work of its own. Its relationship with Makerism follows their shared foundation.

Collegium and corporatist traditions contribute older ideas about association and government. The Threefold Voice comes directly from Generative Sovereignty. America Reborn supplies civic expression, while the fiscal program explains how particular obligations are financed. These roles help a reader follow the reasoning into policy.

Hearthism

Where Makerism begins

Generative Sovereignty begins with the conviction that moral truths are real and that a people must teach the character needed to live by them. Its account of self-government includes the formation of persons and the institutions and work that sustain them. From that starting point, I developed Makerism and Hearthism and brought inherited traditions into the wider framework.

Makerism gives particular attention to the people whose work creates and preserves those conditions. The maker includes the mechanic keeping equipment running and the teacher preparing someone to understand it. A parent caring for a child belongs within the same account. Much of the work that keeps a country alive never appears as the manufacture of a physical product.

We should be able to explain how an economic or political arrangement helps this work continue. An increase in a company's value can accompany the loss of its plant and training. A government can acquire powers while losing the ability to provide an essential service. The gain on paper and the condition of the country need to be examined together.

Makerism treats a person as someone with rights and duties, formed within a household and a place. Conscience and ordinary liberty require protection. The commonwealth must answer for the condition of the people it exists to serve.

Work and ownership

A person who owns a home, a farm, a workshop, or an interest in a productive business has something he can maintain and pass on. Ownership makes responsibility tangible. It gives a family a stake beyond the next wage payment and gives the owner a reason to care about what survives him.

The policybook therefore joins useful work to wider ownership. It protects the room to begin an enterprise and challenges arrangements that let an incumbent control access without improving what it supplies. The small proprietor and the employee both need an economy in which skill and honest effort can lead somewhere.

Large organizations can do work that smaller ones cannot carry. Their scale must be judged by what it enables and by the power it places over other people. An indispensable service demands particular care because the customer may have little freedom to refuse its terms. Competition, public alternatives, and enforceable remedies address that practical dependence.

The duties of public authority

The Accountable State has work that a household cannot perform for itself. It must defend the country, enforce the law, and confront organized predation. A family cannot individually break a cartel or keep a national grid functioning. Public strength is necessary where the task requires it.

That strength brings obligations of its own. Officials must explain the authority they exercise, account for the money they use, and answer for the result. Citizens retain their ordinary liberties and lawful means of challenging those who govern them. The state exists to protect the people and their common life; the condition of its departments cannot become the country's highest purpose.

The Threefold Voice comes directly from Generative Sovereignty. It brings Consent, Community, and Competence into public judgment. People authorize government, regions give standing to the consequences borne by particular places, and makers bring knowledge of the work. Makerism draws on that principle within the wider framework.

How the philosophies fit together

Hearthism is another philosophy I developed from Generative Sovereignty. Its concern with the household explains whom productive life must serve and how people acquire the habits that responsible freedom requires. It has its own argument and standing within the framework.

Collegium draws on older traditions of association and government. Its account of bodies with real duties gives organized work a place in public life. Producerism develops the economic commitments to useful creation, broad ownership, and resistance to extraction. These traditions and philosophies are brought together through the foundation that preceded them.

America Reborn expresses the work in civic language. Fiscal Policy supplies the means of financing the programs and recording their obligations. Each has a particular job. Their relationship should make the policybook easier to understand and its proposals easier to judge.

From purpose to a policy

A policy needs an actor, a means of payment, and an account of what will happen when it is put into practice. Building a factory requires land and equipment, workers who know the trade, and a business able to use the plant. Keeping a family in a home requires attention to supply, ownership, income, and the costs that return every month.

Makerism asks public policy to attend to these conditions. We owe the next generation more than a set of promises. We owe it the practical knowledge and working institutions through which it can continue to govern its own life.

Questions and answers

What is the relationship between Makerism and Generative Sovereignty?

Generative Sovereignty is the foundational philosophy I developed first. It guided the selection of older traditions and the development of Makerism and Hearthism. Makerism addresses a particular part of that work: the people, ownership, and productive institutions through which a country can maintain its independence.

Where does Hearthism belong?

Hearthism and Makerism grew from the same foundation. Hearthism has its own account of family, social life, and public responsibility. It explains the formation and care that productive and political institutions depend on, including obligations a market account may leave unrecorded.

How does an ordinary policy enter this philosophy?

Follow the work the policy makes possible. A factory proposal needs people who can operate and maintain it. A housing proposal should explain how a household can acquire and keep a home. The mechanism must identify who carries those duties and how the arrangement can be corrected when it fails.
